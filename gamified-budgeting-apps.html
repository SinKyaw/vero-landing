Gamified budgeting apps:do they actually work?

By the Vero team•Updated 24 November 2026•5 min read

Short answer

They can, when the game rewards the habit you want, like tracking, planning and saving. They backfire when the rewards encourage more spending, or when keeping a streak matters more than your actual money.

The test is simple: ask what earns you points. If spending more never earns you more, the game is probably on your side.

What “gamified” means in a money app

Gamification borrows ideas from games to make a dull task more engaging. In money apps, the most common ones are:

Common game features in money apps, and what each one is for.
FeatureWhat it looks likeWhat it’s for
StreaksA count of days in a row you’ve logged or stayed on planMaking a habit daily
Challenges“No takeaway this week” or “save £20 by Friday”Small, clear goals
Points or tokensEarned for actions like logging or finishing a challengeInstant feedback
Levels and leaguesMoving up tiers, or ranking against friendsProgress and friendly competition
RewardsGift cards, discounts or prizesA tangible payoff
A companionA character that reacts to how you’re doingMaking the app feel personal

Why it can help

Budgeting usually fails for a boring reason: people stop doing it. The benefits come weeks later, but the effort is needed today. Game features try to close that gap.

  • Small wins feel good now. A ticked challenge or a growing streak gives instant feedback for a habit that usually pays off much later.
  • Clear next steps. “Log today’s spending” is easier to act on than “be better with money”.
  • Accountability. Leagues and shared challenges add a light social nudge.
  • Less shame. A playful tone can make it easier to look at your spending honestly.

What can go wrong

The same techniques can also work against you. Regulators have noticed: the UK’s Financial Conduct Authority has looked at how game-like features in investing apps can push people towards riskier choices.

  • Rewards that need spending. Cashback and discounts are still nudges to buy. They only save you money on things you’d buy anyway.
  • Rewarding activity, not results. Points for opening the app every day don’t mean your money is in better shape.
  • Streak anxiety. Losing a long streak can make some people give up altogether.
  • Hidden incentives. Some apps earn money when you take out a product they suggest. That isn’t wrong, but it should be clear.

How to tell a good one from a bad one

Questions to ask before you download a gamified money app.
AskGood signRed flag
What earns points?Tracking, planning, saving, finishing challengesSpending, or buying through the app
What are the rewards?Optional and unrelated to spending moreOnly usable if you buy something
What happens if you miss a day?A gentle restart, or a way to protect your streakPressure, losses or guilt
How does the app make money?Clear subscription or plainly labelled partnersUnclear, or pushes products hard
Can you ignore the game?The budgeting works without itYou can’t budget without playing

So, do they work?

For many people, game features are the difference between budgeting for a week and budgeting for a year. They work best as a reason to keep showing up, not as the point of the app.

Choose one where the rewards follow good habits, the game is optional, and the budgeting still makes sense without it.

Questions people ask

Are gamified finance apps good for Gen Z?

They can be, because they turn a slow habit into something with quick feedback. Look for ones that reward tracking and saving, not spending.

Do rewards in Vero encourage spending?

You earn tokens for tracking, staying on your plan and finishing challenges, and spending more never earns more. The rewards are discounted gift cards, so they’re best used on things you’d buy anyway.

What if I lose my streak?

Pick it back up. A streak is a tool for building a habit, not a score to protect. The habit matters more than the number.
