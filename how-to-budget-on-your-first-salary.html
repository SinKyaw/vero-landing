How to budget onyour first salary

By the Vero team•Updated 24 November 2026•4 min read

Written by the Vero team. Tax figures are for England, Wales and Northern Ireland in the 2026/27 tax year. Scotland has different income tax bands. Facts checked on 24 November 2026. This is general information, not financial advice.

The short version

  1. Work out your take-home pay from your payslip.
  2. Budget from payday to payday, not by calendar month.
  3. Set aside rent, bills and savings first.
  4. Divide what’s left by the days until payday. That’s your daily spending number.
  5. Track for 30 days, then adjust.

Step 1: Understand your payslip

Your salary isn’t what lands in your account. Before you’re paid, your employer takes off tax and other deductions. Here’s what you’ll usually see:

Common deductions on a UK payslip in 2026/27.
DeductionWhat it is
Income taxNothing on the first £12,570 a year, then 20% up to £50,270, on a standard tax code
National Insurance8% of what you earn between £12,570 and £50,270 a year
Workplace pensionUsually at least 5% of your qualifying earnings. Your employer adds at least 3%
Student loan9% of what you earn above your plan’s threshold. £25,000 for Plan 5, £29,385 for Plan 2

Your tax code is usually 1257L. If it looks different, or ends in M1, W1 or X (an emergency code), check it with HMRC so you don’t overpay.

Step 2: Budget from payday to payday

Most people are paid monthly, often near the end of the month. If you budget from the 1st to the 31st, your money and your plan never line up.

Instead, start your budget month on payday and end it the day before your next one. Everything gets easier when the money and the plan start together.

Step 3: Pay the fixed stuff first

As soon as you’re paid, set aside everything you already know you’ll owe:

  • Rent and bills: rent, council tax, energy, water, broadband.
  • Regular costs: phone, travel pass, subscriptions you actually use.
  • Savings: moved out on payday, not whatever’s left at the end.

A separate account or “pockets” in an app makes this easier, because money you’ve set aside stops looking spendable.

Start an emergency fund early. A buffer of even a few hundred pounds stops a surprise bill turning into debt. Many people aim for three months of essential costs over time.

Step 4: Choose a split that fits

A common starting point is the 50/30/20 rule: 50% on needs, 30% on wants and 20% on savings. It’s a guide, not a rule. With UK rents, needs often take more than half, and that’s normal.

Step 5: Know your daily number

Once the fixed costs and savings are set aside, the rest is your flexible money. Divide it by the days until payday:

£250 food + £380 fun = £630 ÷ 30 days ≈ £21 a day.

A daily number is easier to use than a monthly one. Spend less one day and you have more the next. Overspend and you’ll see it straight away, not at the end of the month.

Step 6: Track for 30 days

Your first budget is a guess. Tracking for a month shows you where your money really goes, and it’s often not where you expect.

  • Log spending as it happens, or check your bank app once a day.
  • Note whether each spend was a need, a want or an impulse.
  • Don’t judge, just notice. Patterns matter more than single days.

Step 7: Review and adjust

At the end of your first month, compare plan and reality. Move money between categories, raise or lower your daily number, and try again. A budget that changes is a budget that works.

Common first-salary mistakes

  • Budgeting your gross salary. Only plan with what actually arrives.
  • Forgetting yearly costs. Set a little aside each month for things like a TV licence, birthdays and Christmas.
  • Lifestyle creep. A pay rise disappears fast if spending rises to match it.
  • Relying on credit to reach payday. Buy now, pay later and overdrafts make next month smaller.

Questions people ask

How much of my first salary should I save?

Saving 10–20% of take-home pay is a common aim. If that’s not realistic yet, start smaller and set it up to move automatically on payday.

Do I pay student loan on my first salary?

Only on earnings above your plan’s threshold. For 2026/27 that’s £25,000 a year for Plan 5 and £29,385 for Plan 2. Repayments are 9% of what you earn above it.

What is the 50/30/20 rule?

It splits take-home pay into 50% needs, 30% wants and 20% savings. It’s a useful starting point, but most people adjust it to fit their rent and goals.

Should I opt out of my workplace pension?

Think carefully before you do. Your employer adds money too, and you get tax relief, so opting out usually means turning down part of your pay.
